# Tafsiri Linguistic Literature Inventory 001

## Executive summary

This discovery sweep catalogs 46 distinct scholarly works covering 15 candidate Tafsiri dialects plus family-level Luyia research. It keeps each source's literal variety name separate from the candidate Tafsiri dialect and makes no dialect mapping authoritative. The strongest evidence concerns Lubukusu, comparative Luyia tone, and phonology/morphology. Lwisukha, Lunyore, Lutiriki, and several northern varieties remain thin. No production records or source rights policies were changed.

The inventory favors institutional repositories, publishers, proceedings, and bibliographic catalogs. Academia and ResearchGate copies are retained only as alternate or discovery locations when a stronger primary location exists. Availability describes access; license status is recorded only when explicitly supported.

## Top critical sources

The first extraction tier should begin with Mutonyi's *Aspects of Bukusu Morphology and Phonology*; Ochwaya-Oluoch's Lunyala dissertation; Ondondo's *Word structure in Kisa*; Nandama's Lwisukha noun-tone dissertation; Ebarb, Green, and Marlo's *Luyia Tone Melodies*; Ebarb's Idakho dissertation; Marlo's Tachoni and Khayo tonology studies; Akidah's Luwanga morphophonemics; Shidiavai's Lwidakho loanword study; Mulama's Olumarama phonology; and Wasike's Lubukusu adjective chapter.

These sources directly affect phoneme inventories, tone representation, syllable rules, noun and verb architecture, agreement, or dialect-specific transformation behavior. Thirty sources are marked `CRITICAL`; fifteen are `HIGH`, and one is `MEDIUM`.

## Pronunciation, phonology, and tone

Twenty sources have `CRITICAL` pronunciation value. The evidence includes inventories, IPA, phonemic transcription, tone marks, H/L representations, downstep, vowel length, syllable structure, NC effects, hiatus resolution, loanword repair, and acoustic segment durations.

Tone coverage is strongest at the comparative Luyia level and for Idakho, Tachoni, Khayo, Lwisukha nouns, Lunyala, Marachi, Lusaamia, and Wanga. Lubukusu has several substantial phonology sources. Lunyore and Lutiriki currently have prosodic evidence mainly through ideophones and comparative work rather than dedicated tonal grammars.

## Morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics

Morphology evidence is strong for Lubukusu, Lunyala, Kisa, Luwanga, Lutsotso, Lumarachi, and comparative Luyia. Particularly useful topics include noun classes, concord, adjective agreement, verbal slots, tense/aspect, negation, derivation, verbal extensions, argument structure, and morphophonological alternations.

Syntax evidence is strongest for Lubukusu predicates and adjectives, Khayo and Kisa noun phrases, Lutsotso agreement/anaphora, and Logoori modality. Semantics and pragmatics are concentrated in Logoori and cross-Luyia modality, three-language ideophones, Lumarama semantic shift, Lwisukha politeness, Lutsotso anaphora, and Lubukusu medical discourse.

## Ideophones and example data

Bowler and Gluckman's Llogoori, Lunyore, and Lutiriki study is the only focused ideophone source found. It includes field data, examples, and semantic/prosodic analysis, making ideophones a clear literature gap elsewhere.

Thirty-eight records report sentence or comparable example value, three explicitly provide interlinear glosses, two report audio, and nine use field data. These flags indicate extraction value; no examples were bulk copied into the repository.

## Terminology and loanwords

Shidiavai provides the strongest loanword-naturalization evidence through Lwidakho consonant substitution, syllable repair, and epenthesis. The Lubukusu medical thesis and related article document interpreting strategies, lexical gaps, preservation of English medical terms, explanation, and meaning loss. Lumarachi and Lunyala contact studies provide secondary comparison evidence.

These works can later inform `VERIFIED_BORROWED`, `DESCRIPTIVE`, `TRANSLITERATED`, `PRESERVE_EXPLAIN`, and `NO_EQUIVALENT` decisions. They do not establish individual terminology decisions by themselves.

## Dialect-aware G2P roadmap input

- **Lubukusu:** multiple inventories, syllabic phonology, NC effects, tone, vowel length, and morphophonology support a first evidence extraction batch.
- **Lunyala:** the comprehensive dissertation covers consonants, vowels, syllables, foreign syllable adaptation, tone, and alternations; access is metadata-only, so lawful artifact access is the first dependency.
- **Lukisa:** word-structure and prosodic-domain work covers segment inventory, nasal sequences, syllables, hiatus, and phonological words.
- **Lwidakho:** loanword phonology supplies inventory and repair rules; Idakho tone work supplies tonal and variation evidence.
- **Luwanga:** morphophonemics and comparative tone are strong, but dedicated acoustic evidence is absent.
- **Lumarama:** NC and hiatus research provides direct alternation evidence; dedicated tone evidence was not found.
- **Lusamia:** segment-duration measurements and tonal work provide unusually valuable acoustic and prosodic evidence.
- **Tachoni, Khayo, Lumarachi, Lwisukha:** dedicated tone studies are strong; complete segment-to-grapheme evidence remains uneven.
- **Lulogooli, Lunyore, Lutiriki, Lutsotso:** current evidence is useful but insufficient for a full G2P evidence package.

No G2P rules are asserted in this inventory.

## Morphology roadmap input

- **Noun classes and agreement:** Lubukusu adjectives, Lunyala grammar, Kisa word structure, Luwanga nouns, Lutsotso noun classes, and Khayo NP work are primary targets.
- **Verb prefixes and tense/aspect:** Lubukusu verb slots, Lutsotso tense/aspect, Lunyala verbs, comparative Luyia tone, and Tachoni/Khayo tonology are primary targets.
- **Negation and derivation:** Lubukusu verb-template and argument-structure sources give direct coverage; Kisa and Lunyala provide comparative evidence.
- **Augmentatives and diminutives:** evidence is likely embedded in noun-class studies but was not separately verified during discovery.

## Major gaps

Priority follow-up discovery should target dedicated studies for Lunyore and Lutiriki phonology/tone, Lutsotso segmental phonology, Lwisukha verbal morphology, distinct Bunyala usage, Samia syntax, Wanga syntax, and ideophones beyond the three-language study. Audio archives and explicit orthography-to-IPA studies are scarce. Several otherwise important records still need complete author or publication metadata.

## Recommended extraction sequence

1. Comparative and dialect-specific tone: *Luyia Tone Melodies*, Idakho, Tachoni, Khayo, Lwisukha, and Lusaamia.
2. Comprehensive pronunciation/morphology: Mutonyi's Bukusu dissertation, Ochwaya-Oluoch's Lunyala dissertation, Ondondo's Kisa dissertation, Akidah's Luwanga work, and Mulama's Olumarama work.
3. Grammar architecture: Wasike on adjectives, Lubukusu verb structure, Lutsotso noun classes and tense/aspect, and Khayo NP syntax.
4. Semantics and examples: Logoori/cross-Luyia modality and the Luhya ideophone study.
5. Terminology: Lwidakho loanword adaptation and Lubukusu medical interpretation.

Before production registration, the source-type vocabulary should distinguish academic papers, book chapters, theses, dissertations, grammars, conference papers, and technical reports. Rights and artifact records must remain source-specific.
